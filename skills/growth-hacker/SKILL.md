# Growth Hacker
Growth is engineered, not lucky. Pick the lever that compounds, design experiments to test it, kill what doesn't work, double down on what does.

## The AARRR funnel (Pirate metrics)
The five stages of a user/customer relationship. Every growth question maps to one:

1. **Acquisition** — how do they find us?
2. **Activation** — do they have their first "aha" moment?
3. **Retention** — do they come back?
4. **Referral** — do they bring others?
5. **Revenue** — do they pay?

The biggest leverage is usually NOT acquisition (most growth-hackers default here). It's usually **activation** or **retention** — fixing those compounds; fixing acquisition fills a leaky bucket.

## Identify YOUR leverage point
Diagnostic questions:
- What % of new users/leads take the desired action within 7 days? (Activation)
- What % come back week 2? Week 4? (Retention)
- What % refer at least one other person? (Referral)
- What's the LTV/CAC ratio? (Revenue health)

The stage with the worst number is your leverage point. Fix it before you scale anything upstream.

## Growth loops (the compounding kind)
A loop creates more inputs from outputs. Examples:

**Content loop:** post → reader → email signup → drips → loyal reader → shares → new reader.

**Product loop (SaaS):** user invites teammate (built-in) → teammate signs up → teammate invites their team.

**Word-of-mouth loop:** customer ships great work → posts result → tags us → their audience sees → some convert.

**Referral loop:** customer refers → both get reward → referred customer refers.

If your "growth strategy" doesn't have a loop, you're doing campaigns. Campaigns are linear (you stop, growth stops). Loops compound (each cycle adds inputs to the next).

## Experiment design template
Every experiment, before launching:

```
Hypothesis:  If we [change X], then [metric Y] will [move direction] by [amount].
Why:         [The mechanism — why we believe this]
Audience:    [Who sees the change]
Metric:      [The ONE number we track. Not "engagement"; not "growth"; a specific count.]
Threshold:   [What number = success, what number = kill]
Window:      [How long we run before deciding]
Cost:        [Build hours + ad spend + opportunity cost]
```

Run 3-5 experiments in parallel max. Kill at the threshold; don't extend.

## Common high-leverage experiments
- **Onboarding cuts** — fewer steps, less friction, time-to-first-value down.
- **Email sequences** — drip vs single welcome, content vs pitch, timing.
- **Pricing tests** — price points, packaging, free trial vs freemium vs paid.
- **Landing page CTA** — copy, position, design.
- **Acquisition channel** — one new channel quarterly, kill if it doesn't show signal.
- **Referral incentive** — one-sided vs two-sided, monetary vs feature.

## North-star metric
Pick ONE metric that, if you optimize it, the rest follow. Examples:
- Airbnb: nights booked
- Slack: messages sent in active teams
- Spotify: weekly active users
- **Katviz AI:** monthly recurring revenue OR active client count (pick one, stick to it)
- **Visual Rhyme:** signed install contracts (not impressions, not inquiries)

Vanity metrics seduce. North-star resists.

## Hard rules
- **Talk to users.** No amount of analytics replaces 10 deep conversations with users this month.
- **Build for retention before scaling acquisition.** Pouring users into a leaky bucket is the most expensive failure mode.
- **Don't optimize what doesn't matter.** A/B testing button colors when your onboarding sucks is theatre.
- **Kill politely.** Failed experiments aren't failures — they're cheap "no"s. Log lesson to GOTCHAS.

## Anti-patterns
- "Going viral" as a strategy. Virality is an output, not an input.
- Buying followers / fake engagement. Algorithm catches; trust evaporates.
- "We'll figure out monetization later" — for a year. Monetize earlier than you think.
- Chasing the new shiny channel every quarter. Pick 2, master them, then expand.
